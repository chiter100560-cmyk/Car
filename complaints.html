The single finding

Not one of these complaints is about attitude. The technician was friendly. The crew was professional and on time. In this market people do not leave bad reviews because you were rude — they leave them because the work was declared finished when it wasn't.

So being nice is not your edge, and neither is being fast — the thirty-minute wax detail read as cutting corners. Your edge is completeness, proven in front of the customer before money changes hands.

Seven places to check before you say "finished"

This list is not theory. Every line is a place a real paying customer in this corridor found dirt after being told the job was done.

  • Between and under the seats — fold the seats down. Do not vacuum around them.
  • Steering wheel — makeup and product residue.
  • Driver's door card and sill — the panel the customer touches every day.
  • The screen — no residue, no smear. If a product marks it, it is the wrong product.
  • Glass corners — the triangle the towel misses.
  • Front bumper and grille — bugs. Florida, so always.
  • Wheel faces — clean, and the dressing actually applied.

Five rules this adds to your standard

  • Nothing on the list is skipped in silence. If a tool breaks or a product runs out, the customer hears it before you start and that line comes off the price. A missing shampoo or an unapplied wheel shine is the single most common failure here.
  • The finish walkaround. You never say "I'm done" from across the driveway. You and the customer walk the car together, and you point at the seven checkpoints above. Payment comes after the walkaround, not before.
  • No upselling at the car. The price is agreed on the phone. If you spot genuine extra work, you mention it as something to book next time — never as a new number while they are standing there with their wallet.
  • Damage is paid for, in full, in money. No brush with metal bristles goes near a wheel, ever. If you damage something, garage keepers cover pays the repair — a free detail is not compensation and offering it is what produced the worst review of the five.
  • Review replies never mention tips or money. Apologise, name the specific thing that went wrong, say what you will do, give a phone number, stop. Arguing in public loses you every reader, not just the reviewer.

Website copy this earns you

One price, agreed before we drive over. If something on your list can't be done that day, you hear it before we start and it comes off the bill. No new numbers in your driveway.

The seats fold down. That's where the crumbs live, and it's the first place most people never look.

We're insured for damage to your car, not just for ours. If we mark it, we pay to fix it properly.

01

The eight complaints, ranked

Ordered by how often they show up across sources. The first three are the ones that cost you money, because they're the ones customers write reviews about rather than just quietly not rebooking.

1

Showed up late, or never said when

#1 cause

"Showed up super late, came unprepared, and didn't do a thorough job. No communication about arrival time."

Late arrival paired with silence is the single most-cited complaint in this trade. Customers forgive a delay. They do not forgive not being told.

Your rule

Text on the way, every time: "On my way, 25 minutes out." If you're running late, they hear it from you before the window closes, never after. Book in 3-hour windows, not exact times, so you're honest about traffic on I-95.

2

Missed spots

Most common quality gripe

"Streaks on the inside glass, didn't box the windows, air vents not dusted."

Not skill — sequence. The same few places get missed by everyone because they're checked last, or in bad light, or after you've mentally finished.

Your rule

A fixed final walk-around, same order every car, with a light in your hand: inside glass (roll windows down two inches — "boxing" — and do the top edge), air vents, door jambs, fuel door, cupholders, seat rails, trunk lip, mirror backs. Never skip it because you're tired. Tired is exactly when spots get missed.

3

The price changed

Trust killer

"Surprise charges. Upsold aggressively without explaining why."

Customers accept a higher price for more work. What they won't accept is finding out about it standing in their own driveway with a half-finished car.

Your rule

Quote in writing before you arrive, tax included. If the car needs more than quoted — heavy pet hair, biological stains, a full paint correction — you stop, photograph it, send the photo, and get a yes in text before touching it. Never a verbal upsell mid-job.

4

"It didn't come out"

Expectation gap

"Permanent stains, deep scratches, clear coat failure, dye transfer, pet odour — still there afterwards."

Some damage cannot be fixed in one visit, or at all. The complaint isn't that you failed. It's that you took the money without saying so.

Your rule

Inspect and call it out before you start. "This dye transfer on the seat is permanent. This scratch is through the clear coat — polishing won't remove it. Everything else I can get." Say it, then text it so it's on record. Under-promise in the driveway, over-deliver on the panel.

5

Streaks everywhere

Florida-specific

"Looked fine wet. Once it dried it was streaked all over."

Direct sun dries product before you can level it. In Coral Springs in August a black hood sits well over 140°F by midday. This is a South Florida problem far more than a northern one.

Your rule

Never work a panel in direct sun. Chase shade around the car, work early or late, or ask them to pull into the garage. Work one panel at a time in heat — not half the car. And do the final inspection in shade, because sun hides everything you missed.

6

"It only lasted two days"

Expectation gap

"Results lasted a day or two and then it looked the same as before."

Customers don't know what a wax lasts versus a sealant versus a coating. They assume whatever you did is permanent.

Your rule

Say the number out loud and put it on the invoice: spray wax ~2 weeks. Sealant ~4–6 months. Ceramic coating years, with maintenance. Naming the lifespan is also your rebooking pitch — it tells them when to call you again.

7

Damaged the car and didn't make it right

Business-ending

"They admitted they caused it and then wouldn't properly compensate."

The damage isn't what ends you. The argument afterwards is. And on a mobile job it's your word against theirs about what was already there.

Your rule

Photograph every car before you touch it — all four corners, wheels, existing scratches, interior. Thirty seconds, every single time, no exceptions. Then after. If you do cause damage: say so first, before they spot it, and let the garage keepers policy do its job. That policy exists for exactly this day.

8

Stopped answering the phone

Reputation killer

"Refund requests went into a queue nobody ever answered."

This is the reputation the whole mobile category carries, and it is the cheapest one for you to beat. It costs nothing to pick up.

Your rule

Answer every call and text, including the angry ones, especially the angry ones. A complaint handled well in public is worth more than a five-star review — every future customer reads how you respond. Reply to every review, good and bad, within 24 hours.

02

The tint trap — and why it's your best opening

What goes wrong with tint

  • Bubbles and peeling — adhesive failure, usually cheap film or a contaminated install. Peeling starts at the edges and spreads.
  • Purple haze — cheap dyed film breaking down under UV. In Florida sun this arrives fast. Buy carbon or ceramic; never dyed.
  • Dust contamination — the outdoor problem. Redoing a bad job costs more labour than doing it right, and doing it outside again produces the same dusty result.
  • Void warranty — major film manufacturers won't honour a warranty unless the install meets their standards.

Here's the opening, and it's a big one

The documented industry pattern: mobile tint operators have no fixed address, so when bubbling or purpling appears months later, the calls stop getting answered right around the time the tint starts failing. Customers know this. It's the main reason people drive to a shop instead of booking mobile.

So make the thing they're afraid of into the thing you sell:

Lifetime warranty on the film and the installation. We're local, we're not going anywhere, and if it bubbles we come back to you and redo it. Free.

You can promise that honestly, because you're buying quality film with a manufacturer warranty behind it and you live ten minutes away. The competitor who can't say that is the one you're taking work from.

Two rules that keep the promise cheap

Ask about the garage before you book a tint job — not when you arrive. "Do you have a garage we can pull into?" If no garage, book the early morning, never a windy or rainy afternoon.

Follow the film manufacturer's install spec exactly, and register the warranty. A warranty you voided yourself is one you pay for out of pocket.

03

The booking script

Most mobile jobs go wrong before you arrive. Ask these five things when they call, every time, and half this page never happens to you.

  1. "Is there an outdoor water tap and a power outlet I can use?" — most homeowners say yes without a second thought. If it's a condo, an office lot or an HOA that restricts runoff, that's your rinseless job, and now you know before you drive there.
  2. "Will the car be in a driveway or a garage?" — decides shade, decides whether tint is even possible that day.
  3. "Will you be home?" — if not, where are the keys, and how do you get paid. Settle it now.
  4. "Anything specific you want dealt with?" — pet hair, a spill, smoke smell. This is where you find the job that needs more than the quoted package, while you can still say so.
  5. "Any damage I should know about before I start?" — invites them to tell you about the scratch, so it can't become your scratch later.

Then send one text, every time

Confirmed: Saturday between 9am and 12pm. Full interior + exterior, $250 all in, tax included. I'll text when I'm 25 minutes out. If the car needs anything beyond that I'll photograph it and check with you first — no surprises.

That one message pre-empts complaints 1, 3, and 4 on this page.

05

The one-page standard

Print this. Both of you follow it on every car, or it isn't a business, it's two guys guessing.

  • Before: photos all round · confirm the quote out loud · point out what won't come out
  • During: never a panel in direct sun · one panel at a time in heat · any change gets a photo and a text before you proceed
  • Final walk-around: inside glass boxed · vents · door jambs · fuel door · cupholders · seat rails · trunk lip · mirror backs — with a light, in shade
  • After: photos all round · say how long it lasts · ask for the review while you're standing there, phone in their hand
  • Always: answer the phone. Reply to every review inside 24 hours.

The one that makes you money

Ask for the review in the driveway, while they're looking at the car — not by text that evening. Review count is the biggest lever you have on Google ranking, and the moment they're happiest is the moment they're standing next to a clean car with you in front of them. Three a week beats anything you can buy.

Tint: Film, Law, Skill

Every type of film on the market and which one to sell — the exact legal percentage for every window on a car and on an SUV — what customers ask for that is illegal, and the words you use to say no without losing the job.

The three answers first

Best film to sell, affordable: carbon as your value line, budget nano-ceramic as your premium line. Two lines, two shades each. Skip dyed (fades purple, kills your reviews in year three) and skip metalised (mirror look breaks Florida's reflectivity limit and blocks GPS and keyless entry).

Legal, measured on the finished window: front sides 28% minimum on everything. Behind the driver: 15% minimum on a car, 6% minimum on an SUV, van or truck. Windshield: nothing below the AS-1 line, ever.

Your 10% roll: legal only on the back side windows and rear window of an SUV, van or truck — and only if that glass is not already factory-tinted. Illegal on any front side window and illegal anywhere on a sedan. Details in section 04.

01

The sentence that wins arguments about darkness

Dark is not cool. Ceramic is cool. A cheap 5% dyed film is nearly black and rejects maybe a quarter of the sun's energy. A legal 35% ceramic rejects around half to two-thirds. If a customer wants the car cooler, the answer is better film, not darker film — and the better film is also the one you make more money on.

02

What to stock — the affordable answer

Line 1

Carbon — the $199–$249 job

Stock two shades: 35% and 20%. 35% is your front-window shade on most cars. 20% covers back sides and rear on sedans. Known value brands in this class: SunTek Carbon / CarbonXP, LLumar ATR, and cheaper trade lines like Global and Johnson. Buy a 40″×100′ roll, roughly $150–$300, which covers about 8–12 cars — so around $25–$35 of film in a $220 job.

Line 2

Nano-ceramic — the $349–$449 job

Stock 35% and 20% again, same logic. Budget nano-ceramic rolls run roughly $250–$700 for 40″×100′. Name brands in reach without a big dealer commitment: SunTek CarbonXP, LLumar CTX. XPEL XR / 3M Crystalline are the tier above — dealer accounts, deposits, and worth revisiting once you are selling ten cars a month.

In Florida this is the line that should sell most of the time. Sell it on the steering wheel and the dashboard being touchable at 2pm, not on looks.

Stock discipline

Four rolls maximum: carbon 35 and 20, ceramic 35 and 20. Every extra shade is money asleep in a cupboard. If a customer wants 50% for a legal-and-light front window, order that one roll when you have sold three of them, not before.

Call a South Florida film distributor and ask what an installer account costs and what the minimum order is. Retail roll prices are not your prices — paying retail is how the margin disappears.

The part that catches beginners

The meter reads glass + film together, not the film

Nobody measures your roll. An officer puts a photometer on the finished window and reads what comes through glass and film combined. Roughly: film VLT × glass VLT. Clear car glass is about 75–80%, so your film always reads darker on the car than on the label.

Glass varies by make and year, so these are close but not gospel. The meter on that car is the only truth. Measure the bare glass before you quote, and measure the finished window before you hand the keys back.

Factory privacy glass changes everything. Most SUVs come with the back half already dyed at the factory, often 15–26% on its own. Put your 20% film over 25% factory glass and the meter reads about 5% — under the 6% SUV floor, illegal, and you installed it. On privacy glass the correct answer is usually no film at all on the back, ceramic on the front two.

Two things the law puts on you, not the driver

The label — 316.2955. You give the purchaser a pressure-sensitive, self-destructive, non-removable vinyl label and affix it to the inside left door jamb. It must state compliance with 316.2951–316.2954, the film's trade name, and your business name. The same statute sets a ±3% measurement tolerance.

The penalty — 316.2956. Any person who sells or installs non-compliant sunscreening material is guilty of a misdemeanor of the second degree. The driver gets a non-criminal traffic infraction. You get the criminal charge. And your business name is on a label inside the door jamb pointing at you.

04

Your 10% roll — where it can legally go

Legal

  • Back side windows and rear window of an SUV, van or pickup — the 6% floor. On clear glass 10% film reads about 8%. Measure it: if that vehicle's glass is darker than average you can still land under 6%.
  • Practice glass. Junkyard windows do not care.

Illegal

  • Any front side window, any vehicle. 28% floor. 10% is not close.
  • Any window on a sedan or coupe. The back is 15% and 10% film reads about 8%.
  • Over factory privacy glass on an SUV. Combined it lands at 2–3%, under the 6% floor.
  • Windshield. Never.

So what do you do with it

Keep it as your practice roll and as legitimate stock for SUV back halves on clear glass. Do not build a price list around it. When you buy your first selling rolls, buy 35% and 20% — those two shades cover almost every legal job in Florida, and the 10% only ever comes off the shelf after the meter says yes.

05

What customers ask for that is illegal

You will hear all of these. Ranked by how often, and each with the answer that keeps the job.

1

"5% limo all around"

Illegal on the front sides of every vehicle, and illegal everywhere on a sedan. Second-degree misdemeanor on you.

Say

"I can do 5% behind the driver on your SUV. The two front windows have to be 28% or the charge lands on me, not you — installing it is a criminal offence in Florida. What most people do is 5% in the back and ceramic 35% in the front, and honestly the ceramic front keeps you cooler than dark film would."

2

"Match my fronts to the factory back"

The single most common request, because SUV privacy glass looks like 15–20%. Matching it on the front sides is illegal every time.

Say

"The factory glass in the back is allowed to be that dark, the front by law is not. I'll put the darkest legal shade up front — it won't match exactly, and any tint shop that says it will match is installing something that gets you pulled over."

3

"Tint the whole windshield"

Full-windshield film below the AS-1 line is flatly not allowed. A transparent strip at the top is.

Say

"Only the strip at the top. But there's a real option — a clear ceramic windshield film. Nearly invisible, legal, and it cuts more heat than the side windows do because the windshield is your biggest piece of glass."

Check the specific product's VLT and that it is transparent and non-reflective before you offer this. It is a genuine upsell and a genuine liability if you get it wrong.

4

"Chrome / mirror / limo-reflective"

Reflectance is capped at 25% on the front sides and 35% behind the driver. Most mirror-look metalised film breaks it, and it kills GPS and keyless entry — which becomes your callback, not the film's.

Say

"Mirror film is over the reflection limit here, and it blocks your key fob and GPS. Ceramic gives you the heat rejection without either problem."

5

"Coloured — blue, red, smoked-amber"

Red, amber and blue are banned on every window.

Say

"Those three colours are banned on cars in Florida. Neutral black or charcoal only."

6

"My friend has a medical exemption, just do mine like his"

The exemption is a real thing under Fla. Stat. 316.29545 — issued by FLHSMV on a physician-signed application, for conditions like lupus and other light-sensitive disorders. It belongs to a specific person and a specific vehicle.

Say

"If you have the FLHSMV certificate, bring it and I'll install to what it allows. No certificate, I install to the legal limit."

Photograph the certificate into the job file before you cut film. Never take somebody's word for it.

7

"Skip the sticker, I don't want it on my door"

Not optional, and it is on you. Fit it every car, every time, before the customer pays.

Say

"It's required by law and it's what shows the officer the tint is legal. It goes in the door jamb where nobody sees it."

8

"Cash, no invoice, just do it dark"

Cash does not change the statute, and the door-jamb label carries your business name. A tint shop with a criminal citation cannot sell detailing either.

Say

"I'd be doing a criminal misdemeanor for a $200 job with my name on the label inside the door. Not doing it — but here's the darkest legal setup and it'll cost you the same."

Do not turn this into a lecture

One sentence about the law, then immediately the thing you can do, then the price. Customers who get told no and nothing else go to the next guy. Customers who get told "no, but here is the version that works and keeps you cooler" mostly buy, and often buy the ceramic.

The skill — what you still have to learn

You have tools and film, so the shopping is done. What remains is reps. Four skills, unequal:

Practise the wall, not the easy part

Do not practise by doing whole cars. Practise rear glass on its own, off the car. Ten rear windows in one Tuesday teaches more than four full cars in a week, and loose glass costs a few dollars from a salvage yard — often nothing from an auto glass shop that is throwing it out.

WK 1–2

Flat glass, no shrinking

Loose door glass on a table. The whole sequence with zero curve: clean, wet, lay, cut, squeegee, edge. Same pane ten times — peel, clean, repeat.

Gate: three in a row with no bubbles, no creases, no specks, edges straight.

WK 3–4

Door glass, in the door

Your BMW, Rasul's car, a junkyard door. Now you work upside down, drop the glass to clean the top strip, and tuck film past the rubber. This is where blades meet door seals and paint.

Gate: four doors of one car under 60 minutes, no light gap, no cut rubber.

WK 5–8

Rear glass — the wall

Four weeks on one window because it is 80% of the difficulty. Shrink on the outside of the glass with heat and a hard card until it is dead flat to the curve, working fingers out from the middle. Only then flip it inside. One piece — a seamed rear window is a complaint waiting to be written.

Gate: five consecutive rear windows, one piece, no creases, under 45 minutes each.

WK 9–10

Five whole cars, free, family only

People who will forgive you. Tell each one you are learning and you will strip and redo it free. Time every car. Photograph every car in direct sun at three days — that is when contamination shows.

Gate: full sedan inside 3 hours and the day-3 photos are clean.

WK 11–12

Five cars at a learning price

$99–$129, sold honestly as a starter rate, written on the invoice: "Learning-rate price. If any window is not right within 30 days I strip and reinstall free." Ask for a review only on a car you are proud of — a review is permanent.

Gate: five paid cars, zero redos. Then full price.

If after week 8 the rear glass still comes out with fingers and creases, buy a 3-day hands-on course out of detailing cash — $1,200–$2,500, with the nearest Florida school in Jacksonville. Worth it after you have struggled, not before: going in cold you spend half the course learning what a squeegee does.

07

Where practice glass comes from

Auto glass shops — best source for rear glass

Replacement shops bin good used glass daily. Walk in, say you are a new installer, ask for their old rear windows. Usually free or $10–$20. Build the relationship anyway: they send you tint referrals, you send them cracked windshields.

U-pull-it salvage yards

Cheap door glass, pulled by you. Call first: what does door glass cost, and will they let you pull a rear window (many will not, it usually shatters). Take a socket set, gloves and a blanket. Pick sedans and small SUVs — what your customers drive.

Body shops and used-car lots

Same pitch, plus a second one: ask whether they want tint on their used inventory at a trade price once you are good. That is how installers get volume without spending on ads.

08

The driveway problem

Tint and open air do not mix

Detailing in a driveway is fine. Tint in a driveway is not. The film is wet adhesive facing the sky for minutes at a time, and wind carries dust, grass, pollen and lovebugs straight into it. One speck is a permanent bubble the customer sees at every red light. You cannot buff it out — you strip the window and redo it unpaid.

Operating rule

No garage, no tint. Ask on the phone before booking: "Do you have a garage I can pull the car into? Tint has to be done indoors or dust gets under the film." Customers accept it instantly because it sounds like a standard — it is one. What you must never do is turn up, see no garage, and do it anyway to save the trip.

What it costs you, honestly

It limits tint to garage homes. In Coral Springs, Parkland and Coconut Creek that is most single-family houses, so the hit is small here — but it is real. It also means detailing stays the volume service and tint is the high-margin add-on. For a no-garage customer: quote the detail anyway, offer tint when they can borrow a garage. Do not lose the detail over it.

Second best if there is truly no garage: early morning, no wind, shade, dry car, a canopy with side walls. That is damage control, not a plan. Your redo rate goes up.

09

When you are allowed to charge

  • Time: full sedan, five windows, under 3 hours, alone.
  • Rear glass in one piece. No seam, no crease, no finger.
  • Clean at arm's length in direct sun, three days after install — not clean in the garage on day one.
  • Edges: no visible light gap from two metres, film tucked, no lifted corners.
  • No damage: no cut rubber, no scratched paint, no broken defroster line.
  • Measured: the finished VLT clears the Florida limit for that window and that vehicle type — and you wrote the reading on the invoice.
  • Label fitted to the inside left door jamb.

The aftercare card is part of the job

New film looks hazy and shows small water pockets for 3–5 days, longer in humidity. Customers who are not warned think you did bad work and review it before it has cured. Say it out loud and hand a card: windows stay up 3 days; haze and small pockets are normal and clear as it dries; no ammonia cleaners; call me before you leave a review if anything still looks wrong at day seven.

10

The three ways a beginner loses money

1

Ripping the rear defroster grid

Stripping old film pulls the printed defroster lines off with it. Repair is a new grid or a new window — hundreds of dollars — and the customer finds out in December.

Rule

No removal jobs until you have practised removal on junkyard rear glass. When you do take them, quote removal as a separate line with its own price, in writing, and state plainly that on old brittle film defroster damage is a known risk.

2

Broken glass, cut trim

Dropped glass, a blade through a door seal, a scratched pillar — all on you, in someone's driveway.

Rule

Garage keepers legal liability active before the first tint car. That is the cover for damage to a customer's vehicle in your care; general liability alone does not include it. It is already on your Step 2 list — do not let the tint start date get ahead of the policy date.

3

Installing illegal tint because somebody asked nicely

316.2956: selling or installing non-compliant film is a second-degree misdemeanor — on you. The driver's ticket is non-criminal. Yours is not.

Rule

28% on front sides, no exceptions, no cash deals, meter on every finished window. Section 05 has the words.

The real money is the combination

Not tint alone — tint sold onto a detail that is already booked. You are at the house, the car is clean, the garage is open, and ceramic adds $350 to a $250 ticket with no second drive and no second sales conversation. A $600 driveway visit that a detail-only competitor cannot touch.

The best-selling package in Florida is the legal one: ceramic 35% front, 20% back, priced all-in with 7% Broward sales tax included. Film transfers to the customer, so the whole bill is taxable — same as detailing.

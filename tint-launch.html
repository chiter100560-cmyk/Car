Insurance — general liability plus garage keepers, written for a driveway

You have the core fact right: general liability excludes damage to "property in the insured's care, custody or control", and the customer's car is exactly that while Alex is inside it. Garage keepers buys that exclusion back. What you don't have yet is the mobile trap and the three questions that decide whether a policy will actually pay.

The mobile trap: standard garage keepers only works at a listed shop address

Ask in writing

The standard form covers a customer's car "left in the insured's care while the insured is attending, servicing, repairing, parking or storing it in your garage operations", and the standard endorsement "applies only at locations on the endorsement schedule". You have no location. The policy must be written or endorsed for off-premises work, or the driveway job is not covered. First question to every quoter: "Does garage keepers apply at customers' residences and other off-premises locations?" Get the answer by email, not by phone.

Three forms. Buy "direct primary" if anyone offers it at a sane price.

Limit matters more than form

Legal liability pays only if you are found at fault. Direct primary pays regardless of fault, like the customer's own collision policy, so a hail, theft or flood loss during hurricane season and a fault fight with a $100k-car owner are both covered. Direct excess pays above the customer's own policy. Direct primary costs 25–50% more than legal liability and is mostly an agent-market product. Whatever the form, the number that matters is the per-vehicle / per-occurrence limit: $100,000 minimum, $150–250k if you will really take $100k cars, deductible $500–1,000. A $50k line does not fix a burned $100k car. You work one car at a time, so per-occurrence and per-vehicle are the same number for you.

What no policy pays for: your own bad work

Faulty work exclusion

Every garage keepers form excludes "faulty work you performed" and "defective parts or materials". Film that bubbles, peels, was contaminated or mis-cut is yours to redo for free, always. What is covered is accidental damage while servicing: a dropped tool dents a hood, a blade slips and scratches glass, the car rolls in the driveway. The grey zone is damage caused by the technique itself — a rear-defroster grid torn off during old-film removal, chemical etching, buffer burn-through — and carriers apply the exclusion differently. Second question, in writing: "Does the policy respond to accidental damage to glass, rear-defroster grids, trim, paint and electronics caused during installation or removal?"

Nobody drives a customer's car until this is bound

Personal auto won't cover it

Moving the customer's car and hitting the garage door is a garage keepers collision claim on their car. Hitting the neighbour's mailbox or the neighbour is third-party liability, and that needs garage liability or hired & non-owned auto (HNOA, $300–700 a year). A personal auto policy excludes vehicles "used in the business of selling, repairing, servicing, storing, or parking vehicles", so Alex's own policy will not defend him or you in a customer's car. Your BMW stays covered on your own policy as your listed car, but tell the insurer it is used for business. Rule until HNOA is bound: reposition in the driveway only, engine on, nobody on the road.

Alex is an employee. Say so on the application.

Do not write "1099 sub"

Florida's workers' comp statute (440.02) calls a worker an independent contractor only if at least four of six tests hold: his own business with his own truck, tools and materials, his own FEIN, paid to a business not a person, his own business bank account, free to work for others, paid per bid or task. Alex works with your film, your tools, your customers and your brand and is paid personally. He is an employee under Florida law whatever the paperwork says. Small-business liability policies carry subcontractor exclusions; if you call him a sub, the carrier can treat his work as uncovered unless he carries his own liability policy. Put Alex as an employee and Rasul as a trainee employee.

Workers' comp is not yet mandatory — Florida requires it at four or more employees for non-construction, and a sole proprietor does not count himself. But liability policies exclude injury to your own employees. If Alex gets a chemical burn or falls off a bumper, nothing pays. Voluntary workers' comp for detailing runs about $100–130 a month in Florida. Price it in the same round of quotes; gated communities' management companies ask for it too.

Google Business Profile — home address, service-area, one-take video

Google's own help pages were readable, so the rules below are Google's words, not a blog's. Most of what you need to do happens before you open the profile. The profile itself takes 20 minutes; the paperwork and props behind it take two weeks.

The rules, from Google

  • Enter your real address, then hide it. "If you're a service-area business, you should hide your business address from customers. For example, if you're a plumber and run your business from your residential address, clear the address from your Business Profile." You still type the real address with the unit number so Google can verify you.
  • Video is what a new home-based service business gets. Google decides the method and you can't change it. Practitioners in 2026 report video as the predominant method for service-area businesses.
  • The video must show three things: your location ("street signs, nearby landmarks, or other identifiers at your business address"), your business ("the professional tools, equipment, or products you use for work"), and proof you run it ("record yourself performing a service or accessing business-only assets", or "business documents like a business permit, invoice, or utility bill").

The number on the box is not the number on the meter. Two "35%" films fail Florida fronts.

Read the spec sheet

The meter reads film × glass, and factory side glass passes 70–80% of light. To read 28% on 75% glass you need a film that actually measures 37%; with the ±3% tolerance (floor 25%) you need at least 33%. Nominal shades vary: Flexfilm Terraflex "35" measures 36% (reads 27–29%), Duraflex "35" measures 34.7% (26–28%, tolerance only), Terraflex "40" measures 42% (31–34%, safe), Tint Depot Ceramic IR "35" measures 36%. Flexfilm Panaflex "35" measures 31.9% and Tint Depot Carbon "35" measures 31%: both read 23–25% on the car and fail. A $100 customer who gets a ticket is a one-star review; put fronts on a film that measures 40–42% and stop worrying about the tolerance.

Rears: sedans need 15% (12% with tolerance): 20% film reads 15–17.5%, fine; 15% film reads 11–12% and fails. Multipurpose vehicles need 6% (3% with tolerance): 5% film on clear glass reads 4–6%, legal only through the tolerance. On factory privacy glass (typically 15–25% already) any 5–20% film stacks to 1–5% and is illegal; meter the glass first and use a 35–50% film there, or leave it. Reflectance is a non-issue: these films reflect 5–10% against a 25% cap.

Enforcement reality: nobody has been prosecuted for it that we could find. Do it anyway.

$1 a car

No reported Florida case or news story of an installer charged under 316.2956(3) turned up. FHP's tint-meter policy (17.27) says nothing about checking door-jamb labels; troopers cite drivers from the meter reading. But FHP's own fleet policy (6.03) requires "a reputable and professional installer who shall affix the label required by Section 316.2955" on patrol cars, so the state treats the label as real. It costs about a dollar and it is the only thing that separates you from the "half the tint blogs say Florida has no sticker" crowd when a customer, a cop or a lawyer asks.

Where to buy: nobody sells a ready-made Florida label. Order custom destructible vinyl.

"SUV / truck" is not the legal test. "Multipurpose passenger vehicle" is.

Check the door jamb

316.2951(2): "a motor vehicle with motive power designed to carry 10 persons or fewer which is constructed either on a truck chassis or with special features for occasional off-road operation." That is word-for-word the federal definition, and every vehicle already carries the answer: the manufacturer's certification label in the driver's door jamb states the vehicle type — PASS CAR gets 15% behind the driver, MPV or TRUCK gets 6%. Photograph that label on every job. A crossover built on a car platform can be certified either way; the label decides, not the shape.

The limits are measured on the glass, with the film on, on that car

Buy the meter

316.2953 and .2954 say "when applied to and tested on the glass of such windows on the specific motor vehicle". Factory side glass is not 100% clear, so a "28% film" reads well under 28% once it's on. Which film shade gives a legal reading, and what to stock, is worked out in section 05. Troopers use FLHSMV-approved meters only: Laser Labs 100 / 200 / 400, Monroe PMP Pocket Detective, New World Instruments Pocket Detective 2.1, Tint Meter Model 1000 (Enforcer II) and 2000 (Inspector II). Own one of those, check every job, write the reading on the invoice.

Medical exemption: it belongs to the car, not the person

See the certificate first

Lupus, autoimmune disease or "other medical conditions which require a limited exposure to light" qualify (316.29545). The owner applies on form HSMV 83390 with a physician's statement, $6.50 per vehicle, and gets certificate HSMV 83391 for that VIN, kept in the glove box, void on sale. A customer saying "I have a note from my doctor" is not an exemption. Ask to see the 83391 before you go darker than the table; the installer misdemeanour has no medical carve-out of its own.
